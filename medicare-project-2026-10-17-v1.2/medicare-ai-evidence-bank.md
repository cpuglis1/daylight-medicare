# Evidence bank: an AI-led Medicare advisor

Research checked October 16, 2026. Prepared around two separate hypotheses: a reliable plan-data and verification foundation, and an AI advisor that performs most consumer-facing education, discovery, selection guidance, and ongoing service. Enrollment execution may retain licensed human involvement.

The evidence supports rising adoption, valuable decision support, and automation of bounded service work. It does not yet establish that an autonomous conversational advisor can replace a licensed Medicare advisor across the full lifecycle. I did not find a comparable longitudinal series establishing that Americans 65+ increasingly trust AI to make Medicare decisions.

**How to read the evidence.** Age 50+ findings are adjacent evidence, not estimates for 65+. “Ever used,” frequent use, stated intention, trust, and authorization to transact are different outcomes. Medicare beneficiaries also include some people under 65. Several AARP publications reuse the same survey; they are not independent confirmations. Company performance claims are labeled. Most decision studies concern Advantage or Part D, so Medigap underwriting and switching rights require separate validation.

**The plan-data and verification foundation**

1. **Chapter’s rationale and current human boundary — CEO interview, March 10, 2026.** The published interview notes describe integrations, machine learning, and AI automation behind provider and prescription-cost tools. They also describe a preference for phone/text engagement and distinguish narrow voice-AI tasks from open-ended guidance, which still uses human advisors. Relevant segments: data engine at 07:37, product design at 17:28, voice AI at 21:46, service model at 41:40. This is founder/interviewer commentary, not a comparative evaluation. The notes do not establish that broad autonomous advice is technically impossible. [Interview and chapter markers](https://www.emergentproduct.com/p/building-revolutionary-technology).

2. **Chapter’s commercial signal — company announcement, April 9, 2026.** Chapter reported revenue tripling in 2025, exceeding $100 million ARR, and flat corporate headcount. It described new provider-directory and prescription-cost products and an AI platform supporting licensed advisors. These are company-reported results; “corporate headcount” is not necessarily total advisor or contractor staffing. No controlled evidence attributes revenue growth or labor efficiency to AI. Its accuracy superlatives are marketing claims. [Chapter’s Business Wire release](https://markets.financialcontent.com/stocks/article/bizwire-2026-4-9-chapter-raises-100-million-series-e-to-continue-building-the-trust-layer-between-seniors-and-technology-in-the-age-of-ai).

3. **Chapter’s infrastructure is concrete — public API documentation.** Its provider API documents geographic/semantic search, provider-location lookup, and provider-plan coverage queries, with authentication required. This verifies productized data capabilities, not the accuracy or freshness of individual records, complete quote retrieval, or availability to a competing startup. [Chapter Provider API](https://providers.docs.askchapter.org/apis).

4. **Personalized comparison can change actual decisions — randomized Medicare Part D experiment, published 2012.** Mailing personalized plan-cost information increased switching from **17% to 28%** and reduced predicted annual consumer costs by about **$100, approximately 5%**. The information already existed for free; bringing it to people mattered. This supports proactively doing comparison work. It was an older Wisconsin experiment with a trusted hospital and other intervention components, not a chatbot trial; the savings were predicted. [Kling and colleagues, Quarterly Journal of Economics](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/127/1/199/1833354?login=false).

5. **Human expertise alone leaves decision errors — Medicare exchange study, 2020 working paper.** Gruber, Handel, Kina, and Kolstad analyzed approximately **59,000 Advantage-with-drug-coverage enrollees**, comparing 2015 and 2017. Their model estimated **$278 better average financial outcomes** after decision support, and enrollment call time fell **21%**. Lower-performing advisors with the tool exceeded top advisors’ earlier performance. Customers were randomized to advisors; the AI rollout itself was not a simple randomized treatment. Dollar results are model-based, not observed cash savings. Humans supplied network considerations the algorithm omitted. Authors disclosed relevant commercial interests. This is evidence for augmentation and consistency, not LLM replacement. [Paper, including methods and disclosures](https://www.ehealthecon.org/pdfs/Gruber_etal_2020.pdf).

6. **Directory listings have needed verification — CMS audit, 2018.** In a review of 64 Medicare Advantage organizations, **52.20% of 14,869 listed provider locations** had at least one error, including wrong location, telephone number, or new-patient availability. This is a historical audit, not a current nationwide error estimate. It supports checking the underlying evidence rather than treating an imported directory as verified truth. [CMS report](https://www.cms.gov/Medicare/Health-Plans/ManagedCareMarketing/Downloads/Provider_Directory_Review_Industry_Report_Round_2_Updated_1-31-18.pdf).

7. **The verification problem persists — HHS OIG, 2025.** A review of selected Advantage and Medicaid behavioral-health networks found inactive providers in directories; many no longer worked at listed locations or would not see the plan’s members. OIG recommended stronger monitoring and directory accuracy. This is a selected behavioral-health review, not an estimate for all physicians or all plans. [OIG findings](https://www.oig.hhs.gov/reports/all/2025/many-medicare-advantage-and-medicaid-managed-care-plans-have-limited-behavioral-health-provider-networks-and-inactive-providers/).

**The consumer problem: comparison friction and continuing service**

8. **Comparison is often skipped — KFF analysis published 2024.** Using the 2022 Medicare Current Beneficiary Survey, KFF found **69%** did not compare coverage alternatives in fall 2021 for 2022 coverage. Among Advantage enrollees, **43%** did not review changes in costs and **44%** did not review changes in covered services. This is historical, weighted survey evidence with specified exclusions. Not comparing does not itself establish a poor decision. Do not compare these percentages directly with newer polls that ask different questions. [KFF analysis and methods](https://www.kff.org/medicare/nearly-7-in-10-medicare-beneficiaries-did-not-compare-plans-during-medicares-open-enrollment-period/).

9. **Recent academic evidence on inattention — Health Affairs Scholar, 2024.** Among **439 standalone Part D beneficiaries** recruited from the Understanding America Study, **53%** reported no plan comparisons. Among non-comparers, **98%** stayed with their plan, versus **67%** among comparers. Reported barriers included difficulty comparing/switching, confusing mailings, and not using advisors or Plan Finder. Fieldwork was December 2023–January 2024 concerning 2024 coverage. This is self-reported, observational evidence, not a causal estimate of an advisor’s value. [Bruine de Bruin and Hodson](https://academic.oup.com/healthaffairsscholar/advance-article-pdf/doi/10.1093/haschl/qxae141/60338202/qxae141.pdf).

10. **Current pain is material but not universal — University of Michigan, October 15, 2026.** National weighted survey of **1,796 adults 65+**, fielded December 30, 2025–January 12, 2026. **36%** reported a coverage-choice challenge; **19%** difficulty comparing information; **14%** estimating total costs. **27%** did no review and **33%** spent under an hour. **34%** knew none of the listed policy changes. However, **96%** were satisfied with prior coverage and **85%** described comparison as easy. A broker/agent was the most important information source for **26%**. Do not equate coverage satisfaction with an optimal choice—or infer universal dissatisfaction. [Full findings and methods](https://ihpi.umich.edu/national-poll-healthy-aging/national-findings/choosing-medicare-coverage-older-adults-views-and).

**Adoption: the audience is changing**

11. **Direct 65+ adoption evidence — Pew, June 2026.** A representative survey of **5,119 U.S. adults**, fielded February 17–23, reports any chatbot use among **65+: 12% in 2024, 23% in 2026**. In 2026, **7%** used chatbots daily and **11%** reported medical-advice use. Among ages **50–64, 42%** used chatbots. Important comparability note: the earlier question used different wording and an awareness screen. These are repeated population surveys, not a panel finding that individuals changed their minds. The age gap suggests an increasingly familiar incoming Medicare cohort; that is an inference, not a forecast. [Pew age breakdown](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2026/06/17/how-opinions-and-use-of-ai-differ-by-age/).

12. **Corroborating 50+ trend — AARP annual technology surveys.** AARP reports generative-AI use among adults **50+ increasing from 9% in 2023 to 18% in 2024 to 30% in 2025**. The latest wave was fielded September–October 2025. Employment matters: **42%** of employed 50+ adults had used generative AI versus **21%** of those not employed. These results support adoption growth, but the 50+ aggregate should not stand in for retirees or people 75+. [AARP’s technology research summary](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/aarp-research-insights-technology/), [latest report and methodology](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/2026-technology-trends-older-adults/).

13. **Health-information use exists among 65+ — Pew, April 2026.** In an October 20–26, 2025 survey of **5,111 adults**, **10% of those 65+** got health information from chatbots at least sometimes. Among chatbot health-information users, **31% of adults 50+** described the information as highly easy to understand, versus **47% under 50**. The latter is a user-only subgroup comparison, not all older adults. This supports both an initial audience and the need to test explanations with older users. [Pew report](https://www.pewresearch.org/science/2026/04/07/users-of-social-media-and-ai-chatbots-for-health-information-are-more-likely-to-say-they-are-convenient-than-accurate/).

14. **Medicare-specific demand and reservations in the same survey — eHealth, October 8, 2026.** September survey of **more than 1,000 Medicare beneficiaries**, conducted through a third-party survey tool; eHealth reports ±3% margin of error. **62% of those intending to review coverage** planned to use AI for comparison; **48% of all respondents** reported prior AI use for plan review. **61%** would favor a person over AI if advice conflicted. **71%** called shopping confusing; **48%** reported delaying/skipping care because of coverage confusion. This is industry-sponsored self-report with limited public methodological detail. The high AI-use estimate differs sharply from broader probability surveys; do not blend them or call 62% the share of all seniors. [Original eHealth release](https://ir.ehealthinsurance.com/news/news-details/2026/Survey-Half-of-Medicare-Beneficiaries-Have-Skipped-Medical-Care-in-the-Past-Year-Due-to-Confusion-Over-Their-Coverage/default.aspx).

**Trust: meaningful interest, substantial conditions**

15. **Health questions are easier to accept than document sharing — AARP, June 2026.** Probability-based NORC survey of **1,031 U.S. adults 50+**, fielded February 12–16, online/phone. Among AI users, **41%** had asked a health question and **62%** expected to within a year. **11%** had shared health documents; **23%** expected to. Among all respondents, **69%** were uncomfortable sharing personal health information/documents with a health-specific AI tool. Among those not completely comfortable, **61%** worried about sharing/sale without consent and **57%** about accountability for harmful guidance. Intentions are not adoption, and the denominators differ. [AARP findings and methodology](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/artificial-intelligence-health-tools/).

16. **Transparency and skepticism — University of Michigan, July/August 2025.** February 4–28 survey of **2,883 adults ages 50–97**, randomly selected and weighted. **55%** had used spoken or typed AI, including Alexa/Siri; this is not an LLM adoption estimate. **14%** had sought health information. **46%** had little/no trust in AI-generated information, while **92%** wanted to know whether information came from AI or a person. Interest in daily AI use was **29%** among those reporting fair/poor physical health, versus **36%** among those in better health. Those with greater need may be harder to reach. [Poll findings](https://ihpi.umich.edu/national-poll-healthy-aging/national-findings/how-older-adults-use-and-think-about-ai).

17. **Useful and free fits stated preferences — AARP’s January 2026 synthesis.** Seven of its top ten AI “jobs” involved health/wellness, including simplifying information and accessing support. Across top jobs, generally **fewer than one in five** were willing to pay directly; many preferred free or insurance-covered tools. But **68%** worried about losing meaningful human interaction and **39%** distrusted AI accuracy/reliability. Findings derive from related AARP research, including a January 2025 online study with **1,148 respondents 50+**, rather than an independent new January 2026 adoption wave. This supports a free, useful service proposition, not proof of demand for autonomous insurance decisions. [Data story](https://datastories.aarp.org/2026/navigating-the-world-of-ai/), [underlying study](https://www.aarp.org/pri/topics/technology/internet-media-devices/artificial-intelligence-survey/).

**Can AI do the advisor’s work?**

18. **Medicare explanations have a technical evidence base — The Gerontologist, 2024.** Researchers tested assistants during summer 2023 using carefully phrased questions drawn from a standardized Medicare questionnaire. Bard and ChatGPT-4 answered terminology questions **100% correctly**; general-content accuracy was **96.3% and 92.6%**, respectively. These results support educational potential. They did not measure real older adults’ comprehension, current plan retrieval, personalized suitability, enrollment execution, or current model performance. The comparison with beneficiaries used an approximately twenty-year-old human sample, not a contemporary head-to-head test. [Langston, Charness, and Boot](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11258897/).

19. **Open-ended answers can help and overwhelm — Human Factors in Healthcare, 2025.** Researchers assessed responses on Medicare, long-term-care insurance, and community resources. Bard’s responses were **6% inaccurate**, versus **60% for Alexa**, across assessed domains. High amounts of supplemental information appeared in **79%** of Bard and **37%** of ChatGPT responses. The study concerns older versions, with original queries collected in 2023; researchers, not a consumer cohort, formulated questions. The product implication is adaptive explanation length and verification, not “AI is 94% safe for Medicare advice.” [Original study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S277250142500003X).

20. **Medicare consumers accept bounded AI service in production — eHealth/Regal case study.** Vendor-reported internal 2025 analysis of after-hours Medicare calls: **77%** rated AI screening exceptional, **100%** answer rate, and **27% higher purchase interest** relative to human screeners. Published methodology does not establish randomized comparable cohorts or completed-policy conversion. The deployment routes qualified customers to licensed agents and now handles status, ID-card, billing-contact, and contact-preference questions. This is relevant behavior, but screening/routine service is narrower than recommendations. It does not establish an exclusively 65+ sample. [Case study and scope](https://www.regal.ai/case-study/ehealth).

21. **Large-scale labor evidence supports assistance — QJE, 2025.** Staggered deployment of generative-AI suggestions across **5,172 customer-support agents** increased issues resolved per hour **15% on average**. Benefits were larger for less experienced/lower-performing workers; some top workers experienced slight quality declines. The humans retained control over conversations. This is one software company, not Medicare and not autonomous service. Use the peer-reviewed 15% estimate rather than mixing it with the earlier working paper’s 14% result. [Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658).

22. **Conversation may help people express their actual need — 2026 preprint.** In the August revision of a tech-support study, LLM query clarification improved automated answer accuracy (**69% versus 35%**); younger helpers understood clarified queries better (**93.7% versus 65.8%**). The diary study included **27 older adults** and a later evaluation **34 older adults**. This supports investigating clarification and context gathering. It is small, concerns technology support, and is not evidence for correct Medicare eligibility or plan selection. The paper’s title and some comparisons changed between versions; these figures refer to v2. [Sharifi and colleagues, current version](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10018v2).

**What this means for the proposed capabilities**

| Capability | Evidence-supported opportunity | What remains to be demonstrated |
|---|---|---|
| Understand the person and establish eligibility | Clarifying conversation may reduce the burden of expressing needs; reliable profile data improves comparison. | Complete capture of relevant facts, consented memory, correct timing/switching logic, and appropriate escalation. |
| Explain choices | Direct Medicare knowledge benchmarks are promising; older adults already ask AI health questions. | Comprehension and recall with actual users, accessible pacing, accurate uncertainty, and correction of misunderstandings. |
| Compare and guide selection | Personalized-information experiments and AI-assisted brokers show value. | An autonomous agent’s suitability versus a licensed advisor using the same verified data. |
| Provide continuing service | Production examples support routine status/contact workflows. | Correct handling of complex coverage disputes, context retention over time, policy changes, and service resolution. |
| Complete enrollment | A plausible bounded execution workflow with licensed review and beneficiary authorization. | Correct applications, effective coverage, handling exceptions, and legal/carrier approval of the actual workflow. |

The primary differentiation hypothesis is that an always-available advisor can turn accurate data into an understood, completed decision, while using much less licensed labor. Better conversational satisfaction alone would not establish this.

**Human involvement may be needed before enrollment.** CMS says Advantage/Part D agents must meet state licensing, annual training/testing, and marketing requirements. Florida, for example, includes recommendations, comparisons, and coverage interpretation within its definition of solicitation when used to persuade a purchase. These sources do not establish a nationwide rule against every automated comparison, or validate a human signoff as sufficient. The required role depends on the state, product, carrier, and actual activity. [CMS](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/health-plans/managedcaremarketing/copy-1-of-agentbroker), [Florida Statutes §626.112](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0600-0699/0626/Sections/0626.112.html).

**Claims the evidence can support**

- Older adults’ AI adoption is increasing; a meaningful initial 65+ audience exists.
- Personalized comparison and decision support can improve Medicare decisions and reduce some advisor labor.
- Some Medicare consumers respond positively to disclosed conversational AI for bounded work.
- Clear explanations, low friction, affordability, transparency, and data protection address measured consumer concerns.

**Claims to treat as hypotheses**

- Trust in autonomous Medicare advice is rising alongside adoption.
- Most beneficiaries would prefer an AI advisor to an available, competent human.
- A contemporary agent can replace the full advisor role with equal or better outcomes.
- Licensed human involvement can always be confined to final submission.
- Lower service labor automatically produces lower total acquisition and servicing cost.

**The most useful validation study to run**

Use one verified data/comparison foundation for both an AI-led experience and a human-led experience. Recruit actual people making a Medicare decision; track ages 65–69, 70–74, and 75+, plus prior AI experience and caregiver participation. Include people who abandon the AI flow in the results. Randomize the experience where feasible, disclose AI, and retain required licensed oversight.

Measure independently reviewed eligibility and recommendation accuracy; missing facts; comprehension of key tradeoffs; time to an informed choice; willingness to share needed information; requests for a human; human minutes per case; effective enrollments; and later corrections, cancellations, unresolved problems, and retention. Compare costs per effective, retained policy, including data access, verification, compliance, escalation, and support. Do not optimize for enrollment conversion at the expense of suitability or comprehension.

Test immediate availability separately from AI identity: a delayed human versus instant AI comparison confounds the value of speed with preference for AI. Also ask whether visible human backup changes completion and trust. Those experiments would directly test the business thesis that the current survey literature leaves open.
